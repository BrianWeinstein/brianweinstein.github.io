I spent four months converting an old rotary phone into a lamp and all I got was this lousy set of AI usage principles

and a neat lamp, and a lot of knowledge & enjoyment. But yes, this is a post about AI and lamps. I am sorry.

Back in June, I was at my parents’ house the day after they’d cleaned out an old storage closet, and among the books and doodads scattered about their kitchen was an old rotary phone destined for the donate pile. I’d been searching for a rotary phone for a few months at that point. I had randomly thought that it’d be fun to make a retro-looking lamp out of one, and so, slowly but surely over the next four months, I did just that. I spent 50-ish hours of my occasional downtime just building this phone/lamp1: I figured out how to wire a Raspberry Pi to the rotary phone’s dial; learned to make a coiled wire using a lightning cable, paracord, and a hair dryer; and overthought2 all the little details. On the surface, I spent an absurd amount of effort just to end up with a mediocre desk lamp. After all, if my goal were to brighten up my desk, I could have just purchased a retro lamp online for $30 and a few clicks. But the point wasn’t to just have a lamp — I didn’t even need a lamp! The point was to make a lamp and really understand how it worked. The process was the point. This process — the slow, methodical digging to really understand something — has been one of the things I’ve loved most about my roles as a Researcher / Data Scientist over the past many years. With the industry’s broad adoption of AI tooling, though, it’s also become what I’m most worried about losing. The judgement and creativity we get to employ when designing studies, interpreting data, and crafting compelling narratives have been at the heart of “research” for as long as it’s existed; and I can’t help but feel a persistent anxiety3 that AI tools are quickly eroding their place in the work. In Research, and in so many other fields, the finished analysis/article/deck/lamp matters, of course, but the real value comes from the hundreds of small decisions that produce it, because that’s where true understanding is created. Really understanding something can’t happen by “taking a shortcut to the center” — it happens “in the detours.”4 None of this means AI has no place in the work — these tools are genuinely good at plenty of things. The challenge is to leverage what they do best without sacrificing the whole point of the work. After much trial & error and countless conversations with friends, I’ve settled on a set of guiding principles that outline when and how I try to use AI in my workflow — making sure these tools serve the process, rather than optimizing it away. I’m sharing the principles here both to hold myself accountable and in hopes that articulating them is helpful to others navigating the shift in their work. My guiding principles for using AI in Research: Only delegate tasks to AI that I’m (mostly) capable of doing myself. If I can’t do a task manually, I usually won’t know enough to judge the quality of an AI’s output. When I do explore something new, I treat it as a learning project and review the results with colleagues who are fluent in that area. Prescribe exactly what to do and how to do it. Rather than handing over the goal and letting the tool run wild, I try to direct it to use specific approaches, steps, and methods, so that I can still find the understanding that “happens in the detours.” Start with a “blank page” and use AI additively, building things up piece by piece. I almost never use AI to generate a full draft to then manually chisel down. Be transparent about methods and take responsibility for the output. I always disclose when and how AI tools were used in the research process5. I only share AI-contributed work after a thorough audit and if I’d be comfortable claiming it as my own. I treat these as guiding principles, rather than rigid rules, and I adhere to principles 1–3 most, but not all, of the time. They don’t fit every situation, and it’s worth deliberately and cautiously deviating from them to experiment as AI tools evolve. In practice, these guiding principles mean I’m comfortable using AI to do things like catch bugs in code I’ve written, convert a one-off code block into a loop, and poke holes in a methodology I’ve already drafted. But I usually avoid using these tools for things like formulating core research questions, writing code or study plans from scratch, and synthesizing data into a final narrative. While the efficiency of AI tooling is tempting, these principles aim to preserve the friction and detours that give research its purpose. Keeping them in the back of my mind ensures these tools serve the craft, protecting the very parts of the process that make the work worth doing. Of course, choosing friction over efficiency comes with its own trade-offs: The phone lamp sits on our bookshelf now, unplugged, because I’m afraid my shoddy soldering will make it explode. And when I do plug it in, it takes three minutes to boot up before the clashing-color LEDs turn on. Any cheap lamp I could buy online would be better, but I’d have no idea how it works. A few reading/watching recommendations on this topic: In Defense of the Detour: On craft, A.I. and what gets lost when we skip the slow part, by Giorgia Lupi in the New York Times. “The greatest contributions to humanity — art, inventions, even products — show evidence of people who got lost in a problem and came out the other side with an idea they would not have found otherwise. That does not happen by taking a shortcut to the center. It happens in the detours.” The Score, by C. Thi Nguyen. Explores how play, craft, and art derive value from “autotelic” struggle, rather than external metrics. Chapters 2, 21, 26, and 27 are most relevant. The Craft of Writing Effectively, lecture by Larry McEnerney at the University of Chicago. A lecture on using writing as a means of thinking, rather than just being a way to convey your ideas. The Biggest Tell That Something Was Written by AI, by Eve Fairbanks in The Atlantic. “When we use AI to flesh out ideas, we lose the most important part of the writing process: thinking… But the effort and the hang-ups are, as they say, a feature of the human thought process, not a bug.” Refusing to accept an AI-poisoned future of journalism, by Marisa Kabas / The Handbasket. “I don’t write because it’s fun (though sometimes it is.) I write because it feeds my spirit. It helps me unspool my thoughts and feelings in the hopes of helping others do the same. The process is the purpose. You don’t have to always like or enjoy the process, but if you don’t respect it enough to do it yourself, there is no purpose.” Will it Lamp? by Clay Mohrman and Rory Shamlian. A bunch of neat lamps. Slop & Shop: Another vertical video, snap-scrolling, content consumption app

But for a cause!

A few weeks ago, I was hypnotically scrolling through a vertical video, snap-scrolling, content consumption app when I encountered a bug: every video in my infinite feed was a “sponsored” post trying to get me to buy something. That same week, there was a new app gaining attention: a vertical video, snap-scrolling, content consumption app in which every video was AI-generated. I paused for a moment and thought to myself, “Am I in hell?” I quickly decided to save that question for another day, then thought, “Why not an app that is… both?” So here, I’m embarrassed to announce my latest and dumbest creation yet: Slop & Shop, a vertical video, snap-scrolling, content consumption app in which every video is an AI-generated advertisement that tries to get you to buy something. The app can only do two things: play AI-generated ads in an endless feed, and (plot twist!) encourage you to make our future less sloppy and less shoppy by donating to a few nonprofits committed to making high-quality, human-created knowledge available to the world, for free. Play around with the app for a minute, then please tap one of the “Shop now”/“Get offer”/“Sign up” buttons to donate to worthy causes. Want to donate without slopping and shopping? Camera 3000: The Camera of the Future

An AI-powered camera app that captures everything in the scene except the important details.

I’m excited to share my new web app: Camera 3000: The Camera of the Future1. It’s an AI-powered camera app that captures everything in the scene except the important details, so the photo you get is eerily similar to reality, but not quite right.2 It just barely “takes a photo.” I was loosely inspired by the “What is a photo?” debate and the rampant overuse of AI. I’m sure there’s something deep to say about both of these, but I have nothing new to add to that discourse. In reality, I just thought it’d be fun to take those ideas to the extreme. How it works: Given a photo, the app first uses the Gemini API to generate a detailed description of the image. Next, the Imagen API uses that description as a prompt to create a new, entirely-AI image of something that only sort of resembles the original scene. How it’s made: In the spirit of overusing AI, I used Gemini/Canvas3 to write most of the app’s code and followed its instructions to get the app live. In the spirit of over-overusing AI, I did almost everything it told me to do, too, including things I knew to be terrible, like publicly exposing my API key4. I’m sure there’s a lesson here somewhere.5 The app could use some polish: The error states are wonky, the UI is barebones, and I’ve given up on allowing a range of aspect ratios for the photo, but alas, it’s just a silly little novelty app. It’s functional, so just have fun with it. Please be kind in your use of this app: I recognize that image generation can be used for malicious purposes, so the app will error out if a user attempts to generate harmful images. All of the AI generated images adhere to the SynthID digital watermarking standard too. Please go try it out! The AI-everywhere future is already here, and it’s partially running on a publicly exposed API key.6 It’s the “camera of the future” in the same way that Dippin' Dots is the “ice cream of the future”: it’s not, and it’s not even good at being the camera / ice cream of the present.
